00 · Carrom

What is Carrom?

Carrom is a tabletop game of Indian origin that is regularly described as a mix of shuffleboard and billiards.

Players use their fingers to strike the “Striker” piece into smaller wooden discs with the goal of knocking it into one of the corner pockets.

The player must strike the Striker from the baseline closest to the player. The baseline is marked on our board with two consecutive black lines.

01 · Introduction

What we set out to build

Project's End goal

  1. Analyze the current board state using computer vision.
  2. Determine the optimal next move by computing the initial position and velocity of the striker.
  3. Move the striker to the desired position and execute the strike.
  4. Simulate the full game while maintaining and updating the game state.

Project Capabilities

  • Uses the RealSense Camera to capture the current state of the board, including the positions of the Striker and all pieces.
  • Finds the most direct shot (i.e., shots that create a straight line Striker → Piece → Hole). If multiple are available, it randomly selects one.
  • The UR7e arm picks and places the striker on the baseline closest to its base and rotates to align for the shot.
  • Activates the actuator to strike the Striker.

Why It Is Interesting

Carrombot is interesting because it combines robotics with a familiar game, which gives us a clear way to test different strategies in a verifiable manner.

Since each shot has a visible result, we can evaluate how well the robot’s vision, planning, and control systems work together.

02 · Design

System design and trade-offs

Design criteria

  • Functional: Detect board pieces, estimate positions, choose a shot, and execute motion.
  • Reliable: Work across lighting changes, board shifts, changing board frictions and repeated trials.
  • Safe: Keep motion within the board/workspace and avoid collisions.
  • Fast enough: Complete planning and execution within a reasonable demo time.

Chosen design

Our chosen design, given a tall enough striker piece, allows for our solenoid and CAD Box to hover above the board. The height is designed to be the same as that of the side bumpers so no pieces will be affected by the CAD Box or the striker.

Design flow

1 Sense

Capture overhead board image and robot state.

2 Perceive

Detect pockets, striker, coins, and board coordinates with the help of an AruCo Tag.

3 Plan

Score legal shots and choose a target trajectory.

4 Control

Convert shot geometry into robot pose and push motion using inverse kinematics.

5 Strike

Activate the solenoid aka striking, observe outcome, and reset for the next turn.

Trade-offs

Since the design of the CAD Box is a certain length, the striker must be tall enough to clear the height of the side bumpers without piece interference.

Engineering impact

The choices we made in terms of design allow for strong repeartability with strong part durability of our custom parts and clean runtime efficiency.

03 · Hardware / Physical Setup

Hardware



UR7e

Lab-owned collaborative robotic arm used for striker positioning and shot execution. The RealSense Camera and CAD Box are fixed to the wrist joint of the arm.

RealSense

Depth camera used to capture board state and find piece positions before each turn.

Solenoid

Actuation mechanism mounted behind the gripper to deliver consistent striker force. A small impact plate is attached to the solenoid plunger so the solenoid can transfer force to the carrom striker more effectively. The plate is epoxied to a hex nut, and the nut is threaded onto the solenoid screw/plunger tip.

Arduino

Connected through ROS2, sends pulses to solenoid through the MOSFET

MOSFET

Allows the low-power Arduino to control higher-powered Solenoid

Electronics

  • The Arduino powers the solenoid indirectly by sending a control signal to a MOSFET driver module. The Arduino output pin is connected to the MOSFET module’s TRIG/PWM input, and the Arduino ground is connected to the module ground.
  • A separate 12V DC power supply provides the actual power for the solenoid. The supply is connected to the MOSFET module input terminals, and the solenoid is connected to the MOSFET module output terminals.
  • When the Arduino receives a pulse-duration command from the main computer over USB serial, it sets the control pin high for the requested duration. This turns on the MOSFET, allowing current from the 12V supply to flow through the solenoid and actuate it. After the pulse time expires, the Arduino sets the pin low, turning the solenoid off.
  • A flyback diode is connected across the solenoid terminals to protect the MOSFET and control electronics from the voltage spike generated when the solenoid is switched off.
  • The Arduino is connected to the workstation via USB, allowing the robot-side software to send shot commands as serial messages specifying the desired pulse length.
  • Connections are secured using WAGO connectors, screw terminals, and soldered joints for improved reliability.
  • Thicker 18 AWG wire was used for the solenoid power connections to safely support the solenoid’s current draw. Standard breadboard jumper wires were too small for the required current, and a breadboard was not used because it was not suitable for the system’s electrical specifications.


Physical Setup



Board Setup

Aruco tag pasted on cardboard that was taped to the bottom-left corner of board (issues with painting on board not being perfectly symmetrical). Ended up using a 7x7 centimeter tag. The striker had to be made tall so that it could be hit by the solenoid actuator. We also used this for point cloud detection of the striker in lieu of correct vision-based striker detection.

CAD Box Design

The CAD Box Design’s main purpose is to support and hold the solenoid. After experimenting with using the grippers as a way to “flick” or strike the striker directly, we realized we would not be able to achieve the needed velocity for the striker to move as required. We then explored the option to use a solenoid to strike the striker. To do this, we still needed full functionality of the grippers, therefore nothing could obstruct their path. With this in mind we repositioned the solenoid so it sits directly behind the grippers. As a result, the rotation of the wrist joint was also simplified so further computation to determine the rotational angle was not needed. To make the Box Design attachable and detachable, we used a bracket system with nuts and bots to be able to fix the box to the wrist of the UR7e arm. To make cable management clean and not affect the board or its pieces, we added compartments and holes to allow wires to run through the box.

04 · Software

Breakdown of Software Pipeline

Overall

Our overall software pipeline consisted of three primary subsystems: vision, strategy, and robot actuation. These components were coordinated through a central control script, which managed the overall game flow and communication between subsystems. The structure of the main control loop was based on the architecture used in Lab 5.

We organized gameplay as a sequence of turns. Each turn was handled by a callback-driven state machine that scheduled tasks onto a job queue. These tasks included moving the robot arm to predefined poses, running computer vision pipelines, computing shot strategies, and activating the solenoid striker mechanism. The callback was repeatedly invoked on a timer until the game reached a terminal state.

Turn Structure

A single turn consisted of five stages:

  1. Vision I: The robot first moved to a predefined “Home” joint configuration positioned high above the board, allowing the overhead camera to observe the full playing area. We then executed the computer vision pipeline to detect the board geometry and determine the locations of all visible game pieces.
  2. Strategy: Using the detected board state, the strategy subsystem computed a desired striker placement and shot direction. This stage determined the target striker position and estimated striking velocity required to execute the shot.
  3. Movement I (Shot Preparation): The robot moved to the striker location, grasped the striker, and lifted it off the board. It then transitioned to a predefined intermediate “Medium” pose slightly above the playing surface to prepare for shot execution.
  4. Movement II (Shot Execution): The robot positioned the striker at the computed target location and rotated the end effector to the desired angle. The solenoid actuator was pulsed to strike the striker.
  5. Vision II: After the shot was completed, the robot returned to the “Home” pose and reran the computer vision pipeline. We used the updated board state to determine which pieces had been pocketed and to update the internal game state and scoring logic.

ROS2 Integration

In addition to the primary turn loop, the main script subscribed to several ROS2 topics that continuously provided state information from other subsystems. These included:

  • The estimated striker pose, published by the depth perception node
  • The detected ArUco marker pose, published by the ArUco detection node
  • The current robot joint states, published through the ROS2 control interface

Incoming ROS2 messages were processed using subscriber callback functions, allowing the system to continuously maintain state information throughout execution.

Vision

The vision component was responsible for detecting pieces on the board and computing the transformation between board and world coordinates. We used classical image processing techniques to rectify the board and detect the board coordinates of the striker and coins. For more precise striker positioning, we implemented a second layer of striker detection using depth point cloud data from the RealSense camera. Coordinate translation was performed using an ArUco tag and rigid body transformations returned by TF.

1. Homography

The first stage of the vision pipeline consisted of rectifying the board into a square image. We tested two different approaches for computing the homography: an ArUco tag-based approach using a marker taped next to the board, and a board-based approach using the geometry of the board itself. We ultimately settled on the board-based approach, although small errors in the homography computation persisted. These inaccuracies caused the computed striker position to be slightly offset from its true physical location.

1a. ArUco Tag–Based Homography

The ArUco tag–based approach used the board’s marker to estimate the board pose and compute a homography transformation. We detected the tag using OpenCV’s ArUco detection pipeline, which returned the image-space coordinates of the marker corners. Since the physical dimensions of both the marker and the board were known beforehand, we constructed a two-step homography pipeline:

  1. Transform from the raw camera image (pixel coordinates) into a rectified board representation in real-world units (centimeters)
  2. Transform from the rectified centimeter-space representation into a fixed-size rectified image in pixel coordinates for downstream processing

These homographies were composed into a single transformation matrix that allowed detected image points to be mapped into board coordinates. This method required minimal image-processing heuristics and was straightforward to calibrate because the marker dimensions were known precisely. However, performance depended heavily on the ArUco marker being perfectly aligned with the board plane. In practice, the marker plane deviated slightly from the board plane, introducing distortions into the rectified image. As a result, this method was not sufficiently reliable for precise robot interaction and shot execution.

1b. Board-Based Homography

The board-based approach estimated the homography directly from the board geometry using classical computer vision techniques to identify the board boundaries and recover the four board corners. We first preprocessed the image using bilateral filtering to reduce noise while preserving strong edges. We then applied Otsu thresholding followed by binary masking to isolate the board from the background.

From the resulting mask, we extracted contours and selected a contour covering at least 20% of the image area as a heuristic estimate for the board boundary. After selecting the board contour, we applied the Hough Line Transform to detect line segments corresponding to the board edges. Since many overlapping line detections were produced, we grouped and merged lines based on spatial proximity and orientation. This produced four dominant lines corresponding to the board edges.

We then computed the intersections of these four lines to estimate the board corners. Using these corner points and the known board dimensions, we computed a planar homography that mapped the board into a rectified top-down view.

This method did not require an external ArUco marker, making it preferable for deployment in a real-world environment. However, the rectified board was often slightly distorted due to imperfect line detection, noisy contour extraction, and the fact that the physical board was not perfectly planar, causing some corners to become partially occluded. Small homography inaccuracies led to noticeable errors in board-to-world coordinate conversion, resulting in significant inaccuracies in the computed positions of the striker and pieces. These errors posed challenges for reliably grasping the striker and calculating accurate trajectories during the strategy phase.

The thresholding pipeline was also sensitive to lighting variation and glare, especially along the reflective borders of the board. To reduce reflections and improve segmentation consistency, we covered the borders surrounding the board with black cloth. Additionally, we placed the board on top of a light-colored surface to improve contrast between the board and the background, allowing the thresholding pipeline to identify the board contour more reliably.

2. Piece Detection

We detected pieces on the rectified board image using the Hough Circle Transform. We used two separate hard-coded parameter ranges for the Hough transform: one for the smaller coin pieces and another for the larger striker. Using separate radius thresholds allowed the detector to ignore unrelated circular structures on the board, such as the corner pockets and printed guide circles.

For each detected circle, we created a binary mask corresponding to the interior of the circle and computed the median HSV color within the masked region. We used median values instead of means to reduce sensitivity to outliers caused by glare and shadows. Detected circles were then classified into piece types using manually tuned HSV thresholds.

This approach successfully detected black and yellow coins across the board in most testing conditions. However, because the striker was physically taller than the standard pieces, its side became visible in the rectified top-down image. As a result, the striker often appeared slightly distorted rather than perfectly circular, although it could still be detected reliably in most cases.

Reliable detection of red coins proved significantly more difficult because they were visually similar to the cue circles printed on the board. Due to this ambiguity, we ultimately excluded red coins from the system.

3. Point Cloud Detection

Similar to the approach used in Lab 5, we used the RealSense depth-based detector to isolate the striker by filtering the point cloud within manually-selected spatial bounds. Since the striker extended vertically above the plane of the board, we selected a narrow range of z-values corresponding to the upper portion of the striker. Additional filtering along the y-axis eliminated points originating from the surrounding workspace.

To improve robustness, we removed statistical outliers from the filtered point cloud and applied DBSCAN clustering to segment the remaining points into connected spatial clusters. Since the striker was expected to appear near its previously detected location, we selected the cluster closest to the estimated striker position. We then estimated the striker location using the centroid of the cluster.

One major challenge was temporal instability in the depth data, since points near the striker edges frequently appeared and disappeared between frames. This caused the estimated centroid to oscillate over time. To stabilize the detection, we maintained a moving median over the previous 50 detected centroids to reduce frame-to-frame jitter. Despite this filtering, the gripper would still occasionally be slightly misaligned with the striker, likely due to residual point cloud inaccuracies. As a result, we often had to manually reposition the striker during testing to avoid damaging it.

4. Board-to-World Coordinate Translation

To allow the robot to interact with the physical carrom board, we converted detected board coordinates into world coordinates relative to the robot base frame. We performed this transformation using the ArUco marker placed adjacent to the board.

An ArUco detection node, based on Lab 7 code, continuously estimated the pose of the marker relative to the camera frame using OpenCV’s ArUco pose estimation pipeline. The node published the marker pose relative to the camera as a ROS2 transform. Using the TF2 library, we transformed the ArUco pose into the robot base frame.

The accuracy of this transformation pipeline depended heavily on the physical alignment of the ArUco tag with the board. In future iterations, we would rigidly affix the tag to the board to reduce alignment error and improve the accuracy of board-to-world coordinate conversion.

Strategy and State Management

State Management

We created Board and Game classes to represent the states of the physical carrom board and the game.

The Board object contained the physical dimensions of the board, including board dimensions, pocket locations, striker region boundaries, and coin and striker radii. It also maintained the current detected positions of all game pieces, which were updated after every call to the vision pipeline. We used these positions for shot simulation.

The Game object contained the state of the game, including the current score and current player. It also computed the score of a turn based on which coins were sunk, and returned flags describing notable events, such as the striker disappearing from view or the red coin being illegally scored.

Strategy

The overall strategy subsystem was designed to combine geometric backward planning for direct shots, forward physical simulation for validating candidate trajectories, and heuristic fallback methods to select effective carrom shots.

To perform the required computations, we wrote a physics module that implemented semi-elastic collision equations using estimated coefficients of restitution, velocity decay under kinetic friction, collision geometry between circular discs, and trajectory propagation under frictional forces. When designing this system, we noted that:

  • During the collision between two circular discs, momentum is only transferred in the direction normal to the plane of contact, so the outgoing trajectory of a struck coin depends only on the position of the striker at the point of collision.
  • We used conservation of linear momentum and partial conservation of kinetic energy, up to a factor determined by the coefficient of restitution, to uniquely determine the outgoing velocities after collision. We ignored factors such as friction during the collision and angular momentum.

Ultimately, our strategy system only considered geometric direct shots as computed by the backward planning algorithm and random shots as a fallback. Although the backward algorithm produced reasonable direct-shot estimates in testing scenarios, errors in board-to-world translation resulted in downstream errors in the actual placement of the striker on the board.

In our final implementation, we did not finish or use the forward simulation. Propagating errors would likely render the simulation results untrustworthy, and our focus shifted toward completing other parts of the system.

1. Backward Planning Algorithm

The backward algorithm was the primary shot-planning strategy used by the system. It worked backward from the four pockets to find direct shots for nearby coins and determine the necessary striker trajectories to accomplish them.

The algorithm extracted all coin pieces from the Board state object and selected target pieces based on the game mode and score state, prioritizing its assigned color in “colors” mode and higher-score pieces in other modes.

For each combination of pocket and target coin, we performed a backward geometric construction to determine the required striker trajectory, consisting of the following steps:

  1. Computing the vector from the coin to the pocket and estimating the velocity needed to overcome friction over that distance
  2. Using semi-elastic collision equations to determine the point of collision and required striker trajectory

Next, we determined the physically feasible starting positions for the striker. Since the striker was constrained to move along its predefined bar near the edge of the board, we searched for valid x-coordinates along this bar from which the striker could reach the collision point.

We considered the following constraints:

  • Maximum allowable shooting angles, implemented as a hard-coded maximum absolute difference of 70 degrees between the incoming striker angle and outgoing coin angle
  • Bounds on striker placement based on the bar length

We computed the feasible region as an interval along the x-axis, then selected the best starting position using a simple scalar cost function. In our implementation, the cost was the absolute difference between the incoming striker angle and outgoing coin angle.

Future work could use a more sophisticated cost function that minimizes the required striker speed and directs the striker away from the pocket after the collision. Using the globally best position, we computed the required initial velocity.

2. Heuristics

In situations where no valid direct shot could be found, we defaulted to a fallback strategy that placed the striker near the center of the starting bar and aimed toward a nearby coin, slightly perturbing the angle so that the coin would be deflected to a different location. This fallback ensured that the robot could still execute a shot and potentially set up direct shots for future turns.

3. Forward Simulation Algorithm

The forward simulation component of the shot-planning system was intended to predict the outcome of a candidate striker shot by simulating the physical motion of all pieces on the board.

Given an initial striker position and velocity, the simulator estimated trajectories and collisions to determine which pieces would ultimately be sunk. Our long-term intention was to integrate this simulator into a search-based planning framework.

Candidate striker initial conditions would be generated through a grid search over possible striker positions and shot velocities. Each candidate shot would then be evaluated by running the forward simulation and scoring the resulting board state.

We used an event-driven approach that maintained estimates for the next possible physical events, including piece-piece collisions, wall bounces, and pocket captures, rather than iterating over fixed timesteps.

The simulation then advanced directly to the earliest event, updated the physical state of the affected pieces, and recomputed only the future events involving those affected pieces. After determining collision events, we applied semi-elastic collision equations to compute post-collision velocities.

Ultimately, we did not integrate this algorithm into the final autonomous strategy pipeline. However, in the future, this could be an avenue worth exploring for more sophisticated shot planning.

Robot Actuation

The actuation subsystem was responsible for translating planned shots into physical robot motion using the UR7e robotic arm and the custom solenoid striker mechanism. Our pipeline combined predefined robot configurations, inverse kinematics-based motion planning, and actuator control. We hard-coded the joint states for two important robot poses: the “Home” pose, from which the robot could see the full board, and the “Medium” pose, used as an intermediate configuration to transition between picking up and placing the striker without hitting other coins.

For interactions involving the striker itself, we used computed world-space coordinates provided by the vision pipeline and then used MoveIt’s inverse kinematics and motion planning modules to compute feasible arm trajectories to the target poses. We considered implementing PID-based control, but because the task did not require fine-grained velocity regulation and there was low error in the arm’s final position, we did not use it.

We encountered issues with MoveIt’s IK-based motion planning, which sometimes generated unintuitive trajectories involving excessive joint rotations or failed to plan a trajectory altogether. We resolved this issue by positioning the carrom board farther from the robot base so that all target poses lay within the arm’s reachable workspace, but in future work, we could set waypoints along a linear trajectory or require that wrist joint angles remain constant.

We hit the striker using the solenoid actuator mounted behind the robot gripper, which was controlled through an Arduino microcontroller connected to the main ROS2 system. The arduino waited for a message containing a pulse time from the COM channel and activated a GPIO pin for the corresponding number of seconds to set off the solenoid striker. After placing the striker on the board, we used inverse kinematics to move the wrist slightly forward in the tool frame, allowing the actuator surface to make contact with the striker. During development, we experimentally determined a linear relationship between actuator pulse length and resulting striker speed by recording striker motion from an overhead camera in slow motion while varying the pulse duration. However, in our final implementation, we did not include varying pulse lengths as a way to reduce complexity during testing.

06 · Conclusion

Lessons learned and next steps

Did We Meet the Criteria?

We met the main project criteria described above. Our prototype was able to detect the board, identify shot information, communicate with the Arduino, and actuate the striker using the solenoid system.

However, we also had some more ambitious goals that we were not able to fully complete. We originally planned to include more advanced strategy code for the robot, including physics-based shot simulation, but the model was difficult to make accurate enough for reliable real-world shots.

Overall, the system worked well as a prototype, even though it was not as polished or complete as we originally imagined.

Difficulties

One major difficulty was dealing with real-world error. Small amounts of noise in the camera detection, homography, robot positioning, and solenoid timing could build up throughout the control loop and cause the final striker position to be slightly off.

We also had hardware and setup limitations. The classroom was too small for a full-size Carrom board, so we tested on a smaller setup. We also had to modify the striker to make it taller, which helped the robot hit it more reliably but was not fully true to the actual game.

Another challenge was modeling the physics of the shots. Friction, contact between pieces, and small changes in force made it hard to predict exactly where the pieces would go after each shot.

Future Improvements

In the future, we would want to improve the strategy system by adding better shot physics, more reliable success prediction, and possibly multi-shot planning. This would allow the robot to choose shots more intelligently instead of only relying on simpler calculations.

We would also want to add closed-loop feedback, such as using the camera to check the robot and striker position before taking a shot. A controller could help reduce error buildup and make the robot’s movements more accurate.

On the hardware side, we would improve wire management, add a circuit enclosure, and use a stronger or more consistent solenoid. These changes would make the setup more reliable, safer, and more polished.
